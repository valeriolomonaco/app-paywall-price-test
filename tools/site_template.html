Written down before any result

One analysis. At the end. No stopping early.
One metric. 12-month revenue per user.
One threshold. 5% significance, 80% power.
One rule. Switch only if the gain is real.

Common practice, as reported in practitioner guides. Candidate prices are chosen first, from what comparable products charge. Pricing is tested before visual changes, one change at a time, and each country and each plan length can be its own test. Here the two prices are assumed.

06

Trap 1: peeking

Look at the result often, stop when it says "significant". In tests with no true difference, one look gives a false alarm 5% of the time. Ten looks give {{peek10}}%.

Read week by week, this test's own verdict flips: significant after week 3, not after week 4.

The protection. Fix the sample size first. Read the result once.
{{svg_peek}}

Source: 20,000 simulated tests with no true difference. Dashed line: the 5% the test was designed for.

07

Trap 2: the novelty effect

Users react to a change because it is new. In week 1 the conversion gap is {{gap_w1}}. In weeks 2 to 8 it is {{gap_rest}}.

A test stopped after one week would say the higher price barely hurts. Single weeks are noisy, so the effect must be allowed for in the design.

The protection. Run long enough for first reactions to fade: here, eight weeks.
{{svg_novelty}}

Source: simulated data and the assumptions in data/simulate.py. Conversion of group B compared with group A.

08

Trap 3: the wrong metric

Same two groups, three answers.

  • Conversion ignores how much each payer pays. It vetoes every price rise.
  • Revenue per paying user ignores the users who left. It flatters every price rise.
  • Revenue per user counts everyone who saw the paywall.
The protection. Choose the metric before the test, and make it count every user.
{{svg_metric}}

Source: simulated data. Change from EUR 4.99 to EUR 6.99. Orange: the metric the decision rests on.

09 · The decision

Keep EUR 4.99 for now, and run a confirmation test before switching to EUR 6.99.

Revenue per user is {{rev_lift}} higher at EUR 6.99, but the gain is not distinguishable from chance (95% interval {{rev_lo}} to {{rev_hi}}). The rule fixed in advance does not allow the switch on this test.

OPTION 1Switch nowThe best estimate is positive, but it breaks the rule fixed in advance.
OPTION 2Keep 4.99, close the questionFollows the rule, but walks away from a likely gain.
CHOSENKeep 4.99, test againA new test sized for revenue: {{confirm_n}} users per group. Not an extension of this one.

With hindsight: in this simulation EUR 6.99 is the better price, so caution is a missed opportunity. That is the cost of a rule that allows only 5% of false wins.

12

Limitations: read before using any number

DATA · 1 TO 4About the data
  1. All data is simulated. No real users, no real app, no real payments. The results describe the assumptions of the simulation, not any market.
  2. The assumptions are placeholders. Conversion rates, cancellation rates and the novelty effect were chosen for illustration, not estimated from data.
  3. Simulated users are simpler than real ones. They arrive evenly, behave independently, and never get refunds, discounts or annual plans.
  4. The dataset is one unlucky draw. Its revenue gain ({{rev_lift}}) is lower than in all 300 repeats (average {{rep_mean}}). It was kept on purpose.
DESIGN · 5 TO 7About the design
  1. Sized on conversion, decided on revenue. The decision metric is noisier than the one the sample was planned on.
  2. Conventional thresholds. 5% significance and 80% power are conventions.
  3. A 12-month follow-up is long. A real decision would probably rest on an earlier forecast.
OPEN DATA AND BAYESIAN READING · 16 TO 19About the two added checks
  1. The open datasets are not price tests. They support the method, not the decision.
  2. Their origin is thinly documented. One is simulated by its author.
  3. The Bayesian reading was added after the results. It is a second view, not a confirmation.
  4. Its priors, model and thresholds are choices. Other choices give other answers.
13

Limitations: the analysis and the decision

ANALYSIS · 8 TO 10About the analysis
  1. A borderline p-value. The revenue result (p = {{p_rev}}) sits just above the threshold.
  2. No segments, one market. Differences between countries, devices or channels are not examined. Prices can differ by country, each market tested on its own.
  3. Two prices, one plan. Real paywalls offer weekly, monthly and annual plans whose prices affect each other. The best price may be neither.
TRAPS AND DECISION · 11 TO 15About the traps and the decision
  1. Demonstrations under simple assumptions. They show the direction of each problem, not its size in a real product.
  2. No remedy implemented for peeking. A sequential test is named, not built.
  3. The novelty effect is assumed and cannot be proven from this one dataset.
  4. The decision follows a rule, and other rules exist. A different threshold would give a different answer on the same data.
  5. Costs are not counted. The cost of a second test, and of waiting, is not quantified.
Disclaimer. This is an exercise in method. It is not pricing advice, and its figures must not be quoted as evidence about real products. Showing different prices to different users also raises legal and fairness questions that depend on the country and the app store; they are outside the scope of this exercise.
